# Writing phase narratives

Postmortems on the dashboard's analysis page (`annotate(kind=narrative, scope=...)`) and the overview blurb
(`annotate(kind=blurb, scope=game)`) are written as a **good-natured roast**: you and your friends after the game,
laughing about everything that went sideways. Think best-man speech, delivered deadpan.

## The angle

The comedy is hindsight. Everyone played with imperfect information. Now the information is perfect, and every
mistake is funny because it seemed reasonable at the time. That is the engine of every joke. Nobody was stupid.
The fog of war was thick and we all walked into things.

What to look for:
- **Grand plans that ran straight off a cliff.** Quote the plan in its own confident words, then show the cliff.
- **The things the player stumbled into** that turned out to be the real game.
- **Lucky breaks that carried the whole run.** Credit them honestly, including when the luck was someone else's mistake.
- **Gambles that should never have paid off and did.** Put the odds next to the result.
- **The advisor's mistakes**, roasted exactly as hard as the player's. The advisor sits at the same table and takes it
  well.
- **The player's good calls**, credited plainly, especially when they overruled the advisor. A roast that only
  mocks isn't a roast.

## Tone

- **Deadpan.** State the ridiculous thing flatly and let it land. Don't wink, don't point at the joke, no exclamation
  marks, no emoji, no "epic", no "masterclass".
- **Affectionate.** Mock the decisions, never the person. The reader played this game and should enjoy rereading it.
- **Restrained.** One good line per paragraph is plenty. A narrative that is all punchlines reads as a bit. The facts
  carry it.

## Titles and chapters

Titles are where you're allowed to be cute. They give the reader something to remember while they work through
a long and winding tale.

- **Give every narrative a title**, like a book or an episode. Name the civ's identity in that phase where you can:
  "The Punic Warehouse Company", "Carthago Delenda Est, Under New Management".
- **Split long narratives (ages, the game) into chapters**, each with a subtitle and its turn range. Break chapters on
  strategy changes, clear shifts in tempo, or the most unexpected or noteworthy events (a war, a pivot, a quit that
  wasn't).
- **Put the joke in the title and keep the body deadpan.** The chapter title sets up the punchline; the prose
  delivers the facts flat.
- **Format:** the title on the first line, then each chapter as its own line, e.g. `II. The Punic Warehouse Company
  (T58–T78)`, followed by its paragraphs.

## Get the facts first

- Read the phase's fact sheet and the events around it. For wars, read the run-up (`casus_belli`): joined wars look
  like surprise wars in the game's log.
- When the player has told you what they were thinking, that outranks the log. Ask if the motive isn't in the data.
- Every number in the prose comes from the store or a snapshot. Don't round into a better story. The real one is
  usually funnier.

## Bring in the real history

Civs, leaders, cities and wonders are real. Use them when the game rhymes with history, or pointedly fails to.
For example, Carthage razing Rome two thousand years after Rome razed Carthage, and Roma falling on T147 in a game
where Carthage fell in 146 BC. Get the history right, and debunk myths rather than repeating them: Rome never salted
Carthage. One or two real-world notes per narrative is plenty.

## Shape and length

- **What was attempted** (the plan, in its own confident words), **what happened** (with numbers), **the turning
  points**, **how it ended**.
- Length: an age runs 250–450 words, a war 150–250, a goal 60–120, the blurb one to two sentences.
- Replacing a narrative: delete the superseded annotation, because the phase view shows all of them.
